Speech What makes a source worth performing

We spent time discussing sources (content) for our interpretive speeches.

We discussed the different types of interpretive speeches: Open, Duo, and Biblical Thematic.

We learned what counts as literature: novels, short stories, children’s literature, poetry, plays, biographies, essays, or other published literary works.

We learned the five “Can I see it?” questions to help us quickly assess potential sources.

  1. Can I see the characters? Can I imagine their voices, posture, personalities, and reactions?
  2. Can I see the scenes? Are there actual moments that could happen onstage?
  3. Can I see the conflict? Does somebody want something? Is something preventing them from getting it?
  4. Can I see change? Does something become different?
  5. Can I see an audience caring? Would someone who has never read the book understand why this matters?

We learned about the five source tests that help us determine if a particular source will make a good interp.

  1. Literary merit: You are borrowing someone else’s words. You should choose words that are worth borrowing.
  2. Personal passion: You are going to live with this piece for months. You should choose stories that you care enough about to keep telling.
  3. Personal growth: You are developing as a performer through this piece. You should choose a story that challenges you to grow.
  4. Redemptive value: You are communicating ideas through the stories you tell. You should choose a story that communicates something worth considering.
  5. Audience impact: You are performing this story for an audience. You should choose a story that gives the audience a reason to care.

We learned about the red flags of choosing a source.

  1. Too much plot — If explaining the story takes longer than performing it, that’s a problem.
  2. Too many essential characters — You may have to eliminate so much that the story collapses.
  3. No playable scenes — Beautiful description alone doesn’t necessarily give you something to perform.
  4. No meaningful conflict — Something needs to matter.
  5. Dependent on the audience already knowing the story — Your interpretation must stand on its own.
  6. Chosen only because it’s popular — Popularity does not equal suitability.
  7. Chosen only because it is funny — Humor is wonderful. But ask whether there is enough substance underneath it.
  8. Chosen only because it seems “competitive” — You have to live with this literature.
  9. “I can’t explain why I care about it.”
