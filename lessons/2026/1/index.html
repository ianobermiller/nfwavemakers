Finding Your Voice

Lesson 1 · Tuesday, September 15, 2026

We had a great first day of club. It was wonderful to see returning competitors and to meet so many new families. Notes for speech and debate went out with the Week 1 email. The passages below are from those notes.

Speech Finding your voice

Your voice isn’t just how you sound. It’s what you have to say. It’s how you communicate it. And it’s the person you become along the way.

What do you like?

  • I love to research something deeply. → Platform
  • Teach people something interesting. → Informative
  • Convince people. → Persuasive
  • Act and tell stories. → Interpretation
  • Think quickly. → Limited Prep
  • Follow current events. → Extemp
  • Discuss theology and difficult questions. → Apologetics
  • Argue, analyze, and strategize. → Debate

Which one immediately sounds like you? Don’t overthink it. Your first answer doesn’t have to be your final answer.

Quick self-assessment. Which describes you best?

  1. I love learning new things.
  2. I have strong opinions.
  3. I love stories and characters.
  4. I think quickly.
  5. I love current events.
  6. I enjoy deep questions.
  7. I like arguing.
  8. I like making people laugh.
  9. I enjoy performing.
  10. I would rather explain something than perform it.

Don’t choose an event because your friend is doing it, someone told you it’s easy, you think it will guarantee you success, it’s shorter than your other options, or you don’t have to memorize anything. There really aren’t easy events. There are simply different kinds of difficult.

Choosing an event that fits you. Ask:

  • What do I enjoy? If you hate your event, you are going to hate practicing, so start with something that you think you’ll have fun doing. And be willing to stretch yourself and consider events that don’t immediately appeal to you.
  • What am I naturally good at? You might as well start with what you know. This isn’t the only reason to choose an event, but it’s a good starting point.
  • What am I willing to work hard at? A person who is willing to work hard will always outperform someone with natural talent who refuses to put in the effort.
  • What would challenge me? If this isn’t your first year, it is time to consider events that you looked past in your first year or two.
  • What do I want to become better at? Speech isn’t just about finding what you are good at. It is about growth and transformation.

Your strengths can change. The shy student can become a powerful speaker. The quiet student can find their voice. The disorganized student can learn structure. The nervous student can cultivate confidence. The argumentative student can learn the art of persuasion. The natural performer can learn patience and humility.

What actually makes a good competitor: preparation, practice, coachability, curiosity, consistency, courage, and character.

  • Preparation. You can’t wing your way through an entire season. Do the assignments. Come to club ready to learn and ready for feedback.
  • Practice. Knowing what you’re supposed to do and actually being able to do it are two different things. Practice at home and in club.
  • Coachability. If someone gives you feedback, your first question shouldn’t be “Do I agree with them?” It should be “What if that is true?” Feedback isn’t an insult. A ballot isn’t a judgment of your worth. Losing isn’t failure. “I lost this round” and “I’m a terrible speaker” are not the same thing. Feedback + action = improvement.
  • Curiosity. If you want to become a great speaker, become an interesting person. A speaker who knows very little has very little to say.
  • Consistency. You become excellent by practicing and making adjustments repeatedly. The student who improves one thing every week will be a very different competitor by the end of the season.
  • Courage. You will mess up. You will lose. You will be nervous. Do it anyway. Courage isn’t the absence of nerves. It’s deciding that something is worth doing even when you’re nervous.
  • Character. Win well. Lose well. Encourage others. Tell the truth. Work hard. Be someone people want to compete with. We’re not trying to create people who will do anything to win.

Your goal this year: don’t just try to win. Try to become better. If your only goal is winning, you’re giving someone else control over whether you feel successful. Give yourself goals you can control. There is a lot of subjectivity in competitive speech. It isn’t like basketball with a concrete score. How we measure success will determine how we feel about and treat ourselves and others during the season.

If you leave knowing exactly what event you want to do, fantastic. If you leave thinking “I have absolutely no idea,” that’s also fantastic. This is the beginning. Try things. Pay attention to what you enjoy, what you’re good at, and what challenges you. Don’t be afraid to change your mind.

Find your event. Find your strengths. Work on your weaknesses. Find your voice. And then learn how to use it.

Debate Our purpose, our goals, our expectations, and our standard

Some of you are here because you love arguing. Some of you enjoy politics and current events. Some of you want to become better speakers. Some of you love competition. Some of you want to learn how to think more clearly. And some of you may simply be here because your parents thought debate would be good for you.

Whatever brought you here, understand something from the beginning: we are not here simply to teach you how to win arguments. We are here to teach you how to think well, speak well, listen well, reason carefully, treat people with kindness and respect, and behave with integrity.

Winning matters. Competition matters. We want to become excellent competitors. But if winning is the only goal, or we adopt a “win at all costs” attitude, we have missed something much more important. Debate is not just about what you can do with your mind or your mouth. It is about what kind of person you are becoming.

Our purpose. As Christians, we believe that everything we do should bring glory to God. Paul writes in 1 Corinthians 10:31: “So, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God.” That includes debate. We are taking the abilities God has given us — our minds, our voices, our ability to reason, our ability to research — and learning to use them well. The question is not merely “How good can I become at debate?” It is “How faithfully can I use the abilities God has given me?”

Our goals

  1. Become better thinkers. A good debater doesn’t simply collect arguments. A good debater learns to evaluate arguments. Truth is more important than winning. Our goal is not to make something appear true. Our goal is to discover, understand, and communicate what is true. We don’t knowingly communicate something false simply because it helps us win. A winning argument built on dishonesty is not a victory worth having.
  2. Become better communicators. Knowing something and communicating it effectively are two different skills. We want to become people who use words well. We want our words to be powerful because they are truthful, thoughtful, gracious, and well-reasoned — not because they are cruel or manipulative.
  3. Learn to disagree well. You can strongly disagree with someone and still treat them with dignity, because every person you debate is made in the image of God. Your opponent is your neighbor. Attack the argument. Don’t attack the person. Compete hard. Honor harder.
  4. Pursue excellence. Just because winning isn’t our only goal doesn’t mean we don’t care about winning. “Whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord and not for men” (Colossians 3:23). When nobody is watching, still do the work.
  5. Be honest. Excellence without integrity isn’t excellence. Evidence is a trust. Read your evidence. Understand its context. Don’t misrepresent the author’s position. Don’t quote something in a way that changes its meaning. If you discover that your evidence doesn’t say what you thought it said, fix it — even if fixing it makes your case weaker. Don’t turn your opponent’s argument into the easiest possible version to attack. Answer the argument they actually made.
  6. Practice humility. You are not always right. Being wrong isn’t the catastrophe. Refusing to admit that you’re wrong is. Winning is not your identity. A trophy measures what happened at a competition. It does not measure your worth as a person.

Our expectations. Work. Prepare. Listen. Challenge yourself. Respect your teammates and your opponents. Accept correction. Take responsibility when you mess up. Compete with integrity: tell the truth, use evidence honestly, represent your opponent fairly, follow the rules, give credit where credit is due. Don’t cheat. Don’t manipulate. Don’t make excuses. Don’t sacrifice character for a trophy. And when you fail — and you will — own it, learn from it, and keep going.

Our standard. Philippians 4:8 starts with “whatever is true.” Micah 6:8 asks us to do justice, love kindness, and walk humbly with God. You will not always win. You will not always be right. You will not always get the ballot you hoped for. But if you walk in integrity, you can go home knowing you competed honestly, represented your opponent fairly, gave your best, treated people with respect, told the truth, and honored God with what you were given.

Let’s compete to win. But let’s never become so determined to win that we are willing to sacrifice truth, humility, kindness, or integrity to get there.
