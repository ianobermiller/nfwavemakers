### Speech: What happens, why it matters, what the audience experiences

This week in club we discussed:

- **Plot** — What happens
- **Theme** — Why it matters
- **Scenes** — What the audience experiences

#### Six elements of plot (story mountain)

1. **Exposition (the beginning)** — Introduces the characters, setting, situation, and sometimes the theme. Ask: What does the audience need to know before the conflict begins?
2. **Inciting incident (the problem/conflict begins)** — An event introduces the conflict and sets the story in motion. Ask: What happens that changes the character’s ordinary life?
3. **Rising action (things get complicated)** — Events build tension, obstacles, and suspense. Ask: What makes the problem harder to solve?
4. **Climax (the turning point)** — The most intense moment, when the conflict reaches a decisive point. Ask: What is the moment everything has been building toward?
5. **Falling action (the aftermath)** — The events following the climax move the story toward a new normal. Ask: What happens because of that turning point?
6. **Resolution (the new normal)** — The story reaches its conclusion, showing how the conflict and characters have changed. Ask: How are things different from the beginning?

Remember: these are not six equally long sections. Some stories spend a great deal of time building tension and only a few sentences resolving it.

#### Topic vs. theme

A topic tells us what a story deals with. A theme communicates an idea about that topic.

- **Topic:** Friendship. **Theme:** True friendship requires sacrifice.
- **Topic:** Courage. **Theme:** Courage means acting even when you’re afraid.
- **Topic:** Forgiveness. **Theme:** Forgiveness can interrupt a cycle of resentment.
- **Topic:** Greed. **Theme:** The pursuit of wealth can destroy what matters most.
- **Topic:** Identity. **Theme:** Our worth is not determined by how others see us.

Remember: your theme is not necessarily a moral you announce to the audience. Often, the strongest interpretation allows the audience to discover the theme through the story itself.

**Finding your theme.** Complete the following thoughts about all of your potential sources.

- My story is about: (Identify the topic.)
- My story explores the idea that: (Write a complete thought.)
- The character learns or discovers: (Describe the change.)
- I want my audience to leave thinking about: (Identify the intended impact.)

Ask: which moments in your story show these ideas? The theme you choose will help you determine the scenes you’ll keep.

#### Choosing your scenes

**Every scene must earn its place in your interpretive speech.** Scenes may serve several important purposes:

- Introducing essential information.
- Establishing a character.
- Revealing a conflict.
- Increasing tension.
- Showing a character making a choice.
- Demonstrating change.
- Developing the theme.
- Creating meaningful audience impact.

Remember: a scene can be wonderful in the book and still be unnecessary in your performance.

### Speech homework

**Story map**

1. Identify your working source: record the title, author, and event.
2. Write your theme: use a complete sentence rather than a one-word topic.
3. Complete your six-part plot map:

- Identify the exposition, inciting incident, rising action, climax, falling action, and resolution.
- For each story element, answer “What happens in my source?”
- Start by mapping the story as it currently exists. Don’t worry about how long the book is. Don’t start rewriting the author. Just identify the important events.
- Next, look at your chosen theme. Circle the events that most clearly communicate that theme. Could a smaller part of this book tell the story you want to tell? Look at subplots as well as the main plot.

**Open Interpretation:** Look for a complete, coherent storyline that one performer can communicate clearly.

**Duo Interpretation:** Consider the conflict and relationships that allow both performers to contribute meaningfully to the story.

**Biblical Thematic Interpretation:** Use the same story-analysis process for individual literary selections, while also considering how each selection contributes to the larger Biblical theme. A Biblical Thematic piece does not have to follow one continuous plot across all its sources.

**4. Identify 5–8 promising scenes.** Move from understanding your story to finding the actual pages you may want to perform. Look at your potential sources. We still aren’t writing a script. We are going to do an inventory of sorts, identifying promising scenes. Ask:

- What is the central conflict?
- Where does the conflict become more difficult?
- Which moment changes everything?
- Would the audience understand this scene without reading the whole book?
- Which scene best communicates your theme?

**Tag scenes.** Go through your source and use these letters to quickly tag important story/plot elements. You can use tabs and/or highlighters.

- E — Exposition: Introduces essential characters or circumstances.
- I — Inciting Incident: Starts the central conflict.
- R — Rising Action: Develops obstacles and tension.
- C — Climax: Contains the major turning point.
- F — Falling Action: Shows consequences.
- RES — Resolution: Reveals the outcome or new normal.

You might also use these additional tags:

- T — Theme: Particularly meaningful to the message.
- P — Performance: Strong dialogue, characterization, humor, or emotional possibilities.

You may find several possible scenes for the same plot element. That’s fine. Today we’re identifying possibilities, not making final cuts. For each scene, record:

- Page or chapter location.
- What happens.
- Which plot element it supports.
- Why it may belong in the interpretation.

**5.** Identify one scene you love but probably don’t need. Explain why you might leave it out.

**6.** Write a 100-word summary of the story you want to *perform*. This should describe the storyline you plan to communicate, not necessarily the entire book.

Students should not begin rewriting or extensively cutting the author’s words yet.

### Debate: The four levels of refutation

How to systematically dismantle an argument.

Remember: you do not have to prove that everything your opponent says is wrong. You have to identify which part of their reasoning fails and explain why that failure matters.

#### Anatomy of an argument

Claim, warrant, evidence, and impact.

- **Claim:** What are you arguing? The statement or assertion that the debater wants the judge to accept as true.
- **Warrant:** Why is your argument true? The logical reasoning that explains why the claim is true or why the evidence supports it.
- **Evidence:** What proof supports your argument? The facts, statistics, research, expert testimony, or examples used to substantiate an argument.
- **Impact:** Why does this argument matter? The consequence or significance of the argument, explaining why it matters to the debate and the judge’s decision.

**Level 1: Claim attack.** Is their central assertion actually true? A claim attack directly challenges the accuracy or validity of the conclusion the affirmative wants the judge to accept. A basic claim attack disputes the affirmative’s conclusion. An advanced claim attack identifies specific flaws, exaggerations, or unsupported assumptions in that conclusion and explains why the affirmative’s central assertion is false, misleading, or insufficiently established.

**Level 2: Warrant attack.** Does their reasoning actually support their conclusion? A warrant attack challenges the logical connection between the evidence or supporting reasons and the claim the affirmative wants the judge to accept. A basic warrant attack questions whether the conclusion follows from the reasoning. An advanced warrant attack identifies the missing assumptions, flawed logic, or unsupported causal connections that prevent the affirmative’s reasoning from establishing its claim.

**Level 3: Evidence attack.** Does their evidence actually prove what they claim it proves? An evidence attack challenges the credibility, relevance, accuracy, or sufficiency of the information the affirmative uses to support its argument. A basic evidence attack questions the quality or reliability of the supporting evidence. An advanced evidence attack identifies specific weaknesses in the source, methodology, context, or applicability of the evidence and explains why those weaknesses undermine the affirmative’s argument.

**Level 4: Impact attack.** Even if their argument is true, how much does it actually matter? An impact attack challenges the significance of the affirmative’s predicted consequences or their importance relative to competing impacts. A basic impact attack questions the magnitude, probability, or timeframe of the predicted consequences. An advanced impact attack compares the affirmative’s impacts with competing consequences, demonstrating why the affirmative’s claimed benefits or harms should carry less weight in the judge’s final decision.

Evidence can be accurate but insufficient. Possible evidence challenges include:

- Whether the source addresses the current security situation.
- Whether the historical example is sufficiently similar to the present case.
- Whether the author is making a general observation or a specific prediction.
- Whether the evidence accounts for existing safeguards.
- Whether the source supports the likelihood of the outcome or merely establishes that it is possible.

None of these concerns automatically discredits a source. The negative must explain why the limitation matters. The best evidence attacks identify the precise proposition that the evidence fails to establish.

Impact refutation can involve:

- **Magnitude:** How severe would the consequences actually be?
- **Probability:** How likely is the complete chain of events?
- **Timeframe:** Is the harm imminent or dependent on uncertain future events?
- **Scope:** How many people or institutions would be affected?
- **Comparison:** Would the affirmative plan create other, more significant risks?

#### Refutation scripting

Start using this pattern when attacking an argument made by your opponent. This does not replace proper tagging. Use this pattern in addition to your regular tagging. This will improve the organization and clarity in your speeches. Use all 5 prompts when attacking an argument.

1. **Identify:** Their argument depends on…
2. **Attack:** The problem with that reasoning is…
3. **Develop:** Explain the weakness with clear analysis.
4. **Connect:** That means their claimed benefit…
5. **Conclude:** Therefore, the judge should…

### Debate homework

Go to your 5 available Monument affirmative briefs and highlight every statement that establishes a threat, every statement that predicts a consequence, and every statement that explains why instating the affirmative plan accomplishes the advantages, justifications, or solves the harms. Then ask yourself which of those three categories has the strongest evidence — and which has the weakest.

Choose 3 significant arguments per brief to work on. That means 15 responses over the week. Two or three a day is a good pace.

For each argument, develop, using the refutation scripting:

1. One claim attack.
2. One warrant attack.
3. One evidence attack, identifying what source material would need to be checked.
4. One impact attack.
5. A paragraph explaining which response you would prioritize and why.
