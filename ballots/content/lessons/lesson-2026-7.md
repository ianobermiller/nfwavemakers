### Speech: Three dimensions of characterization

This week we learned how to bring characters to life in our interps. We discussed how to create distinct, believable, and consistent characters by understanding their motivations and making deliberate vocal and physical choices.

A character is more than a voice. A character is a person with desires, fears, personality, and a unique way of responding to the world.

1. **Internal characterization — Who is the character on the inside?** Consider their personality, desires, fears, values, motivations, and past experiences. Think about the difference between a character who is angry because someone betrayed them and a character who is angry because they’re afraid. Understanding *why* a character feels something helps the performer make more believable choices.
2. **Vocal characterization — How does the character sound?** We discussed pitch (how high or low a voice is), volume (how loud or soft a voice is), rate (how fast or slow you speak), rhythm (the flow, pattern, or cadence of your speaking), tone (the attitude or emotion communicated with your voice), and accent (any pronunciation associated with your character).
3. **Physical characterization — How does the character carry themselves?** The way we move our body can convey different things. Posture communicates confidence, fear, age, and attitude. Facial expressions communicate emotional reactions. Gestures communicate personality and intentions. Mannerisms communicate unique character habits. Movement communicates energy, confidence, and hesitation.

Characterization is not simply making voices or faces. It’s understanding a character well enough to portray them convincingly. The strongest characterization begins with understanding who the character is and what they want.

Remember: understand the character. Make deliberate choices. Be distinct, believable, and consistent.

### Speech homework

If you have selected literature:

1. Consider your characters, starting with the most important ones.
2. Identify their motivations, fears, and personality traits.
3. Experiment with vocal and physical characterization. Make deliberate choices about both.
4. Read a short section aloud in character, even if it hasn’t been cut yet.

If you are still selecting literature:

1. Choose two characters from a familiar story.
2. Create distinct vocal and physical characterizations for each.
3. Practice switching between them while keeping their personalities consistent.
