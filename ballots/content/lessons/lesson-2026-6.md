### Speech: Three goals of a good cut

We learned a lot this week! Here is the recap:

**Three goals of a good cut**

1. **Clarity — Can the audience understand what is happening?** A scene might make perfect sense to someone who has read the book but be confusing to someone encountering the story for the first time.
2. **Cohesion — Do the scenes fit together?** A collection of funny, dramatic, or emotional scenes is not necessarily a complete story.
3. **Impact — Does the material communicate the intended meaning?** A shorter script should still give the audience a reason to care.

#### The five cutting tools

The NCFCA Foundations of Speech curriculum identifies five tools that can help us shape an Interpretation script. These tools allow us to work with the author’s language while improving the clarity and flow of our performance. They are not permission to rewrite the story however we want.

**Tool 1 — Combine phrases from several sentences.** This allows you to remove unnecessary material while preserving important information.

**Tool 2 — Add single words from nearby sentences.** This allows you to clarify transitions or identify characters. Sometimes, after removing a sentence, the next sentence becomes confusing. We may need a word to help the audience understand who is speaking or what is happening. NCFCA permits adding individual words from nearby sentences for transition or character identification. It does not permit collecting isolated words from throughout a passage to manufacture entirely new sentences.

**Tool 3 — Repeat the author’s words.** This allows you to create emphasis or strengthen a moment. We emphasized the author’s existing language. Repetition can increase tension, reinforce a theme, or emphasize a character’s emotional state. Repetition should be purposeful. If you repeat everything, nothing stands out.

**Tool 4 — Make limited grammatical changes.** This allows you to maintain clarity and grammatical agreement after material is removed or rearranged. NCFCA allows changes involving pronoun substitution, verb tense, and subject-verb agreement. Removing characters or combining passages may require limited grammatical adjustments. The goal is to preserve the author’s meaning while making the resulting script understandable. We aren’t changing the story. We’re making the cut function grammatically.

**Tool 5 — Reassign dialogue and turn narration into dialogue.** This allows you to reduce unnecessary characters, create more opportunities for characterization, and make a passage more engaging to perform while preserving the author’s original words and meaning. This tool has two related applications: reassigning dialogue from one character to another existing character, and turning narration into dialogue by allowing an existing character to speak words originally written as narration. NCFCA does not allow the interpreter to invent a new character or freely rewrite the author’s language.

**Reassigning dialogue to another character.** Sometimes a character serves a very small purpose in a story. They may deliver one important piece of information but contribute little else. Rather than portraying another character for just one line, we may be able to give that line to an existing character. But we must be careful. Reassigning a line should not distort the characters, relationships, or meaning of the original story.

**Turning narration into dialogue.** This is one of the most interesting applications of our cutting tools because it can transform a passage from something the audience simply hears described into something they experience through a character. Remember, Interpretation is not just reading literature aloud. We want to bring the author’s words to life. Sometimes an author tells us what a character is thinking, feeling, or experiencing through narration. We may be able to use those same words as dialogue. Turning narration into dialogue requires careful grammatical decisions, not merely adding quotation marks.

When should you turn narration into dialogue? Just because we can turn narration into dialogue doesn’t mean we always should. Narration serves an important purpose in Interpretation. It establishes setting, explains events, and provides context. Dialogue allows us to experience characters interacting, making decisions, and expressing their emotions. Keep narration when establishing a time or setting, explaining important background information, connecting scenes, describing events the audience needs to understand, providing clarity efficiently. Consider dialogue when revealing a character’s thoughts, expressing an emotional realization, showing a character making a decision, bringing a character’s reaction to life, creating a meaningful performance opportunity.

#### Making scenes connect

Ask yourself these questions for every transition:

1. **Where are we?** Does the audience understand the location or change of setting?
2. **When is this happening?** Does the audience understand whether time has passed?
3. **Why does this happen next?** Does the audience understand the connection between events?

Sometimes the author’s existing narration can connect scenes. Sometimes a transition can be shortened using the permitted cutting tools. And sometimes we discover that we removed something we actually needed. When you’re creating your Interpretation script, you must find the necessary connections in your source and work within the permitted cutting rules to answer these questions for your listeners. A successful cut preserves the audience’s ability to follow cause and effect.

Cutting literature is not simply an exercise in shortening. It is an exercise in storytelling. Every time you remove a sentence, combine a phrase, or eliminate a character, you are making a decision about what your audience will experience. Your job is to preserve the story’s essential meaning while making it possible to perform.

Remember: keep the conflict. Preserve the meaning. Protect the author’s voice. Make every word count.

Come up with 5 unique 15-second hooks for the following prompt: **The Value of Failure.** Your hooks should fall into each of the following categories: Suspense, Question, Story, Shocking Statement, and Humor. Do not use AI. You may briefly research if necessary.

Examples using the prompt “Why people follow crowds”:

**Suspense:** Imagine you’re at school when suddenly everyone starts screaming and running toward the exits. You have no idea what’s happening, but your heart starts racing, and you run too. You don’t even know what you’re running from, but you’re terrified.

**Question:** Have you ever pretended to like a song just because all your friends loved it? Or laughed at a joke you didn’t even think was funny? Why do we care so much about doing what everyone else does?

**Story:** One time, all of my friends started saying 67 and doing this stupid hand motion. I thought it was so annoying. But a week later, guess who was saying it every five seconds? Me. And I still don’t even think it’s funny.

**Shocking statement:** There’s something that can make you ignore your instincts, betray your beliefs, and do things you’d never dream of doing alone. It doesn’t need to threaten you or force you. Sometimes, all it takes is a crowd.

**Humor:** Have you ever stood in a line without knowing what it was for? Maybe it’s free food. Maybe it’s concert tickets. Maybe it’s the bathroom. But hey, if twelve people are waiting, there must be something worth waiting for!

#### The Broken Trophy

Olivia stared at the shattered trophy lying on the floor of her brother’s bedroom.

She hadn’t meant to break it.

She had only come into his room to borrow a book. The trophy had been sitting near the edge of his desk, and when she reached across the desk, her elbow struck it.

The trophy had fallen to the floor and broken into several pieces.

Olivia knew how much the trophy meant to her brother. He had spent months preparing for the competition, and winning first place had been one of the happiest moments of his life.

She heard footsteps approaching the bedroom.

Her brother appeared in the doorway.

“What happened?” he asked.

Olivia looked at the broken trophy. Then she looked at her brother.

She thought about telling him that the cat had knocked it over.

“I broke it,” she whispered.

Her brother stared at the pieces on the floor.

For a long moment, he said nothing.

Then he sighed.

“I wish you’d been more careful.”

“I’m sorry,” Olivia said.

He knelt beside her and began gathering the pieces.

“Come on,” he said. “Maybe we can fix it.”

#### 3. Creating your first working script (for your interp)

This week it is time to begin working with your own literature.

1. Gather the scenes identified in [last week’s homework](/lessons/2026/5).
2. Begin arranging those scenes in a logical sequence.
3. Use the five cutting tools to shorten the material.
4. Identify transitions that need attention.
5. Read the developing script aloud to evaluate clarity and pacing.
6. Bring a working draft to the next class, even if it is still too long or incomplete.

**Cutting each scene**

- Identify the purpose of the scene. Explain why the scene belongs in your interp and how it contributes (plot, theme, or character development).
- Mark lines that must remain.
- Cross out details that appear unnecessary.
- Identify confusing transitions.
- Look for opportunities to combine phrases.
- Identify unnecessary characters.
- Note any grammatical changes that may be needed.

**Test each cut.** Read your shortened passage quietly or to a friend or family member. Ask:

- Does it still make sense?
- Can someone unfamiliar with the book understand it?
- Does it still sound like the author’s story?
- Did I accidentally remove the reason the character behaves this way?

This is a first draft, not a polished tournament script. Students should retain their source text and a record of their changes so that they can verify their cutting choices against the current NCFCA rules.
