### Speech: Conflict in a story

This week, we had some fun playing games that help us think on our feet, we learned the importance of conflict in a story, and we practiced impromptu speaking. If you struggled, that’s okay. Speaking in front of people, especially when you haven’t been given much time to prepare, can be very scary. Keep trying every week.

**Character + want + obstacle = conflict**

Want creates action. Obstacles create conflict. Conflict forces characters to make choices.

While you are considering literature this week, ask these questions:

- Who wants something?
- What do they want?
- Why do they want it?
- What stands in their way?
- What do they do about it?
- What happens if they don’t get it?
- Can I imagine performing that struggle?

Think about your characters. List each of the main characters in your sources and fill in the blanks.

- My character is: __________
- My character wants: __________
- Because: __________
- But: __________
- So the character: __________
- If they don’t get it: __________

Then imagine the scene. Is it taking shape? What could we actually watch this character do?

Look for moments where someone wants something badly enough to do something about it. Those moments give you characters to portray, conflict to perform, scenes to build, and ultimately a story for your audience to experience.

**Who wants what, and what is stopping them?**

Want → action → obstacle → choice.

#### Platform speeches and the SCORRE method

We presented platform speech ideas and had an opportunity to get feedback. The best, most effective platform speeches have strong focus, good organization, a clear reason that you chose that topic, and an impact that makes the judge care.

Coach Shannon teaches from [*The Secrets of Dynamic Communication*](https://www.amazon.com/Secrets-Dynamic-Communications-Prepare-Deliver/dp/0849921902) by Ken Davis, using the SCORRE method.

Before you begin writing your speeches, you must have a strong, focused Objective Statement. This is similar to a thesis statement. However, a thesis statement will go into your speech. An Objective Statement narrows and defines your speech before you write a single word. Your Objective Statement must have a keyword. That keyword will determine your rationales.

Once you have an Objective Statement, you can write your rationales. Rationales are the 3 main points of your speech. The rules of your rationales are:

1. The rationale must correspond to the key word.
2. The rationale should be brief.
3. The rationale should be parallel in grammatical form.
4. The rationale must prove your objective statement true.

Once you have your rationales, you can start deciding on your resources. Resources are the illustrations, data, anecdotes, and humor that will bring your speech to life. Resources make our ideas visible, credible, personal, relatable, and memorable. The purpose of our resources is to:

1. Bring light, color, and clarity.
2. Make the audience want to listen.
3. Clarify and strengthen your rationale.
4. Be relevant to the tone and focus of your speech.

The general outline of your speech should follow this pattern:

- **Introduction** — Introduces your topic, includes all important background information necessary for understanding the concepts in your speech, and articulates your roadmap.
- **Rationale 1** — Your first rationale that defends your thesis using resources.
- **Rationale 2** — Your second rationale that defends your thesis using resources.
- **Rationale 3** — Your third rationale that defends your thesis using resources.
- **Conclusion** — Concludes and wraps up your points, reminds the listener of the most important elements, and helps the listener understand why it should matter to them and, if persuasive, what action they should be moved to.

Judges typically ask themselves 4 invisible questions during your speech:

1. Why should I care?
2. Can I understand this?
3. Can I trust you?
4. What’s in it for me?
